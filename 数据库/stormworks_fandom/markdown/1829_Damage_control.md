# Damage control

| [![Stub](https://static.wikia.nocookie.net/stormworks_gamepedia_en/images/f/f1/Stub_v1.png/revision/latest/scale-to-width-down/64?cb=20250514003444)](https://static.wikia.nocookie.net/stormworks_gamepedia_en/images/f/f1/Stub_v1.png/revision/latest?cb=20250514003444) | *This article is a [stub](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Category:Stubs). *You can help Us by [expanding it](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Damage_control?action=edit). |

Vehicles can be damaged in Stormworks in many different ways. In the base game, there is the risk of colliding vehicles with other vehicles or terrain. Additionally, an engine which runs too fast and is inadequately cooled can overheat and catch on fire. Fire can spread to nearby walls and components causing gradually spreading damage. There are also some [missions](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Wiki/Missions) which create substantial risk of vehicle damage, e.g. towing an old sea mine to a port. With the [Search and Destroy DLC](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Search_and_Destroy_DLC), enemy vehicles may also fire guns, rockets, and missiles at player-made vehicles. With the many different ways vehicles can be damaged, it is good practice to prepare vehicles to be able to survive the damage and recover.

## Damage propagation

Damage can spread by fire, by ammunition cookoff, or by warhead detonation.

Damaged batteries and engines can catch on fire. The fire can spread across anything, even normal blocks, unless/until is extinguished. A single uncontrolled fire can eventually destroy an entire ship or aircraft.

Damaged ammo drums which contain ammo can cook off. Several rounds of whatever ammo type they contain will be shot out in random directions around the drum. Large quantities of ammo packed together can easily cause a rapid chain reaction.

Torpedo, missile, and bomb warheads can explode if they are damaged. If they are positioned near each other and one is damaged, it can lead to almost instantaneous detonation of the entire group. EMP warheads will also trigger their EMP effect if damaged.

Rocket launchers, battle cannon belts, artillery cannon belts, and Bertha cannon belts are all safe to damage without risk of chain reaction.

## Bulkheads

Dividing a large ship or submarine into several sections separated by walls along its length and width can help to contain flooding.

### Collision risk

The front of a ship is the area most likely to be damaged in a collision, so the front bulkhead area is generally the most important. To prevent fires it should not contain batteries, ammo, or fuel-driven engines. Additionally, it needs to be sized appropriately. If it's too small, the bulkhead may be damaged along with the hull, leading to further flooding behind it. Thus, it needs to be designed large enough to contain the damage but not overly large so that it remains easy to bilge out if it floods.

## Fire detection

Temperature sensors can detect the added heat of a fire. Once the sensor is broken, it will suddenly report a temperature of exactly 0. A high temperature or 0 temperature can both be taken as signs that an area is likely to have an active fire.

## Fire suppression

At the very least, nearly every vehicle should carry a fire extinguisher. A small upgrade from this for ships likely to have large fires to put out is to include a water pipe running down the length of the vessel supplied by a large pump. With numerous hose attachment points on this pipe, a player can attach a hose to one of the points and use it as a fire extinguisher with unlimited supply.

Where possible, components most likely to catch on fire if damaged, like diesel engines and batteries, should have automatic fire suppression systems. This can be accomplished with fluid cannon nozzles set to a wide spray, or with fluid cannons configured with micro-controllers to automatically spray an area. Temperature sensors can automatically detect that the area is hot (or has been damaged, which would mean probably on fire) and trigger the fluid cannons to douse the fire.

## Repair

Repair can be done with by hand with welders or underwater welders, or with a vehicle using mounted welders. Most vehicles should have at least a welder and underwater welder for basic manual repairs. Mounted welders use large amounts of electricity but can be run semi-autonomously, e.g. placed on a track and moved down the the length of a ship to weld everything damaged along the hull. They can also reach places that are too small for a player to get into, e.g. a 2x2 tunnel through a ship could fit a compact rail and several welders that weld everything in all directions around the length of the tunnel.

### Lingering effects

Even after being repaired, batteries will still be depleted. Ammo drums that cooked off will be depleted of ammo.

Note that fluid tanks and gas tanks will *not* lose their contents if damaged. Also, warheads that were part of a vehicle/subvehicle which was not too damaged to continue existing can be repaired and detonated again.

## Redundancy

For critical systems, like power, it's generally a good idea to have a redundant backup.

For example, note that a damaged battery will still have zero power after repair. If you needed the battery to start your engine and needed the engine to charge your battery, then you may be left with an inoperable vehicle that you can't get working again. Having a backup battery in a separate location and a circuit breaker to protect it against being drained will allow you to fix your damaged main battery, restart your engine, and then recharge both batteries.

### Manual backups

Hand cranks can be a very useful way to either manually crank an engine to start (if the starter has insufficient power), or to provide initial charge to a battery so basic systems like throttles, buttons, clutches, and starters can be used.

Be aware that clutches do not work without power and they are commonly used to limit power taken by generators. If there's only one battery and one generator and the generator takes power from the engine through a clutch then in the event of battery damage there will be no way to get the vehicle running again unless/until it can be jumpstarted. For this typical situation, it is good practice to have one or two handcranks connected to the generator *after* the clutch.

Note that hand cranks turn slowly, usually less than 1 rpm, but have decent torque. Three [gearboxes](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Wiki/Guides/Gearbox) set to 3:1 with the arrows facing towards the handcrank(s) and a small generator are an effective way to recharge a small battery enough to start an engine within a few minutes.

### Auxiliary power units (APUs)

See [auxiliary power unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_power_unit) at Wikipedia.

Many real-life aircraft carry an engine and generator solely dedicated to providing power for the aircraft. As the name suggests, this is a dedicated backup system. Generators connected to the main engines provide all power under normal circumstances, although the APU may be used to initially power up the aircraft before takeoff if ground power is unavailable. Further, in the event of simultaneous engine failure, the APU can be switched on in-flight to maintain power to control surfaces, fuel pumps, landing gear hydraulics, de-icing systems, and life support to buy time for the engine failure to be diagnosed and corrected, or, if all else fails, to at least facilitate an attempted [deadstick landing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deadstick_landing).

For Stormworks, much of the same logic applies. If an aircraft has just one battery at the bottom of the hull and it gets hit by a single round of ammo from a rotary autocannon, the engines will likely shut down in most designs, because:

- If a throttle is used to control engine throttle, an unpowered throttle will suddenly output 0, resulting in engine shutdown.

- If a clutch is used, an unpowered clutch will stop working resulting in total loss of power transmission from the engine to the rotor / propellers.

- If any electric [pumps](https://stormworks.fandom.com/wiki/Pumps) are used (e.g. pumps acting as superchargers/turbochargers), these will shut down, blocking all air flow to the engine.

In addition to likely loss of engine power, all control surfaces will shut down, landing gears will be inoperable, pumps for fire suppression systems will not run, and mounted welders will be unable to repair the situation. With a backup battery, electric relay, backup generator, and appropriate microcontroller, the aircraft could automatically get power back on, extinguish the fire(s), and repair the main battery along with any other critical systems protected by mounted welders.

Note that a basic APU can be made with a small diesel engine, a gearbox set at 3:2 (arrows facing the engine), and a medium generator. The generator will produce 36 swatts while the engine will run at 8.16 rps and consume 0.06L of diesel per second. The power is a bit more than enough to run one mounted welder at a time.
